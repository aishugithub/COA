Unit 2: Processing Units – Detailed Teacher’s Notes
Learning Objective: To master the internal mechanics of the CPU, specifically understanding how bits are transferred between registers, manipulated by the ALU, and controlled by either hardwired or microprogrammed logic
.
I. Register Transfers and Register Transfer Language (RTL)
References: Mano Ch. 4, pp. 93–102; Hamacher Ch. 2, p. 33
Before complex instructions execute, the processor performs microoperations: elementary operations on data stored in registers
.
Register Transfer Notation (RTN/RTL):
The Concept: A symbolic language used to describe the internal movement of bits
.
Notation: R2←[R1] denotes copying the contents of register R1 into R2
.
Control Functions: A transfer is typically synchronized with a clock and governed by a control signal P. This is written as P:R2←R1, meaning "If P=1, then transfer R1 to R2 at the next clock edge"
.
Bus Transfers (Hardware Implementation):
To avoid a "web" of wires between n registers, a Common Bus System is used
.

Logic Microoperations:
Operations like AND, OR, XOR, and NOT are performed bit-wise in parallel
.
Applications to Highlight:
Selective-Set (OR): Sets specific bits to 1
.
Selective-Clear (AND with complement): Clears specific bits to 0
.
Masking (AND): Clears a portion of a word while preserving the rest
.
Shift Microoperations:
Logical Shift: Moves bits and inserts 0 into the vacated position (shl,shr)
.
Arithmetic Shift: Multiplies/divides a signed number by 2. Arithmetic Shift-Right (ashr) is critical as it preserves the sign bit to maintain the number's polarity
.
Rotate (Circular): Bits shifted out of one end are inserted into the other (cil,cir)
.
III. Memory Transfers: Fetching and Storing
References: Mano Ch. 4, p. 101; Hamacher Ch. 5, pp. 166–167
The processor-memory interface relies on two specialized registers: the Memory Address Register (MAR/AR) and the Memory Data Register (MDR/DR)
.
Read (Fetch): DR←M[AR]
The processor puts the address in AR and issues a Read signal. The memory transfers a copy of the word at that address into DR
.
Write (Store): M[AR]←R1
The processor puts the target address in AR and the data in a register (like R1 or DR) and issues a Write signal, overwriting the memory location
.
IV. Execution of Complete Instructions (Five-Step RISC Sequence)
Reference: Hamacher Ch. 5, pp. 155–168
For a uniform RISC-style processor, every instruction is broken into five distinct clock cycles
:
Step 1 (Fetch): IR←[[PC]]; PC←[PC]+4. The instruction is fetched and the PC is incremented to the next word
.
Step 2 (Decode/Read): The Control Unit decodes the OP-code while simultaneously reading source registers from the Register File into inter-stage registers RA and RB
.
Step 3 (Compute): The ALU performs the operation (addition, logic, or effective address calculation). The result is stored in register RZ
.
Step 4 (Memory Access):
For Load: Read memory at address RZ into register RY.
For Store: Write data from RB into memory at address RZ.
For Arithmetic: Simply transfer RZ to RY
.
Step 5 (Write Back): The result in RY is written into the destination register in the register file
.
V. Multiple Bus Organization (CISC Style)
Reference: Hamacher Ch. 5, pp. 178–182
While RISC uses a fixed pipeline, CISC processors often use a flexible Three-Bus Organization
:
Structure: Buses A and B provide two operands to the ALU inputs simultaneously, and Bus C returns the result to the registers in the same cycle
.
Incremental Steps: CISC instructions vary in length. For example, a complex instruction like And X(R7), R9 (ANDing a memory location with a register and storing it back) requires seven steps, including multiple memory accesses for the offset X and the operand
.
VI. Microprogrammed Control
References: Hamacher Ch. 5, pp. 182–184; Mano Ch. 7, p. 213
Instead of a fixed logic circuit (Hardwired Control), a microprogrammed unit uses software-like logic inside the hardware
.
Control Word/Microinstruction: Each bit in a control word represents one control signal (e.g., "Read Memory" or "ALU Add")
.
Control Store: A special internal memory that holds microroutines—sequences of microinstructions for every machine instruction
.
Microprogram Counter (μPC): Tracks the address of the next microinstruction to be fetched from the Control Store
.
Execution: During the instruction's decode phase, the starting address of its microroutine is loaded into the μPC. The processor then steps through the microroutine until an "End" bit is reached, triggering the fetch of the next machine instruction
.
Teaching Summary: Unit 2 bridges the gap between Unit 1's functional blocks and the actual physical flow of data. Emphasize that every high-level line of code eventually becomes a series of register transfers orchestrated by the Control Unit through either hardwired logic (speed) or microprograms (flexibility).
.

### **Teacher’s Strategy Note**
In building these notes, **Morris Mano** was used for the **gate-level logic** (how an arithmetic circuit is physically wired), while **Hamacher** was used for the **organizational flow** (how an instruction moves through timing stages in a modern processor). This approach ensures students understand both the "how" (hardware) and the "when" (control/timing).
